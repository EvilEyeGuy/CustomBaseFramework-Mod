Settlements

Settlement

A settlement plan defines what a place needs: its building mix, fortifications, districts and defenders. The generator uses those requirements to arrange a village, town, fortress or camp on the actual map. You control the composition and placement preferences; the terrain and available space determine the final positions.

A settlement type is built from three layers:

  • Wards are the rings of the place: the old town in the middle, the suburb around it, the countryside outside.
  • Districts are groups of buildings inside a ward, for example a market quarter or a row of farmsteads.
  • Entries in a district name a category or a single piece and how many of it to build.
  1. List of settlement types, grouped by their lowest tech level. The number on the right is the weight. A red row has errors that stop the type from being built; it shows the number of errors instead of the weight.
  2. Preview. Off by default, because planning a large town takes a few seconds. Show preview draws the plan on a flat map, Reroll plans it again with a new seed, Wards colours the rings.
  3. General: name, kind of place, tech levels, factions and more. See General.
  4. Wards on the right: one tab per ring of the settlement. See Wards.
  5. Faction overview below the list shows which faction gets which settlement, see Factions.

The buttons at the bottom right:

  • Save writes the settlement type into its file.
  • Build finds a matching tile on the world map and places a settlement of this type there.
  • Check plan plans the settlement type 30 times on flat land, rivers, mountains, coast and a worst case. See Check a plan.
  • Preview opens the preview large in its own window, with legend and tally.
  • Show loot shows the loot of the settlement of this type that was built last. See Loot of a built settlement.

Settlement types from CBF or other mods can be changed too. The save button then reads Save as patch and stores your changes as a patch in your own mod, see Changing content of other mods. Copy into my mod instead makes an independent copy under a new name.

Check a plan

At the top of General, a coloured line estimates the space the plan needs: green fits, yellow is tight, red is probably too big. It is a quick guess from the minimum counts and updates with every change. The tooltip lists the details.

Check plan gives the reliable answer. It takes one to a few minutes and can be cancelled. The result shows:

  • how many of the 30 runs were valid, with a traffic light: green from 90 %, yellow from 60 %, red below, and the result per terrain
  • the quick estimate next to it, because both can differ
  • per ward and district, the entries that stayed below their minimum most often, in red when it happened in at least half of the runs
  • the notes of the planner, such as a wall that does not fit or a map that is too small

If an entry is left out often, lower its minimum, set its Priority to Low or move it to a ward with more room.

Make a small variant first

Use Copy into my mod on a small plan whose building set matches your active mods. Give the copy a distinct name, then change one district: for example, add a workshop category with a count of 1~2.

Preview the plan and reroll it a few times. The workshop should appear among that district's buildings where space permits. If the plan struggles to fit, reduce the building requests before adding further wards or fortifications. Build a test map when the layout looks right; the preview is useful for placement, while the map lets you inspect furniture, loot and defenders.

Keep faction assignment in mind when you finish. A valid plan still needs an eligible faction or a quest that explicitly selects it. See Factions.

Loot of a built settlement

After Build, the button Show loot shows what the loot of the settlement built last is worth in silver: in total, per ward and per kind of goods. The value is counted while the settlement is built and saved with it. Medieval Overhaul containers that only fill when searched are not included.

What the generator handles by itself

You choose the buildings and rules; CBF handles the roads and adjustments needed to fit them together. The following behaviours explain some differences between the plan and the generated map.

  • Buildings that belong at the wall, such as barracks, dock onto the town wall with their open side. They are only built where that side touches the wall. The wall stays and the building gets a door onto the walkway.
  • An open side of such a building that does not touch the wall is closed with the building's own wall.
  • With the default grown outline, a medieval old town uses overlapping blocks to create an irregular boundary. This also holds for towns without suburb islands if the wall is thicker than 3 cells. Every straight wall stretch, every projection and every indent is at least 16 cells long (a third of the town width for small towns).
  • If there is no room for a field right at a farmhouse, the field may lie up to 30 cells away. When no road passes it, its gate faces the farmhouse.
  • Wall parts cut off by a mountain turn into rock. Large rock pockets behind the wall get a postern (a small side gate). Small rock islands that touch the planned wall stay on the wall line and outside the town and replace the wall there; only the part inside the town is removed.
  • Rubble inside the settlement is cleared.
  • Modern towns always have wards shaped like a spider web: 7 to 10 corners with sagging sides, also with a sandbag or barricade line. They never get gatehouses; roads run through gaps.
  • Medieval settlements at the coast or on a wide river get a harbor with jetties, fishing huts, harbor store, harbor master and boat sheds, linked to the town by a path. The settlement type lists the harbor buildings under harbor, see Settlement types in XML.
  • Buildings whose graphic only has one direction, such as Medieval Overhaul carts, are never rotated.
  • Stoves, ovens and workbenches face into the room, and every thing gets a material it can be made of.

General

The section has four sub-tabs. Basics: what the place looks like (label, kind, material list, sizes, suburb, ideoligion style, build as ruin, world map icon). Placement: who gets it and where it appears (tech levels, weight, factions, excluded, conditions). Garrison: how many defenders it has. Mods: required mods, takeover, World Domination, A Rim of Ice and Fire and camp types. Errors and the space check stay above the sub-tabs.

Garrison

  • Minimum points: one value. The place always spends at least this many points on defenders, however poor your colony is.
  • Garrison factor: 0 to 5, decimals allowed. A factor on your colony's raid points, the points the storyteller would spend on a raid right now. The place spends the raid points times this factor, but never less than the minimum points, also past the usual 10,000-point cap of raids. The shipped places are tuned so that at 11,200 raid points a village has about 30 and a fortress city about 100 defenders, for example a fortress city with 3,000 minimum points and a factor of 0.37. With 0 the garrison entries work as fixed counts as before. Points are taken when you enter the map.
  • Pawn anchors count against the budget: 0 to 100%, 50% by default. If fixed pawns and counted anchors already cost the whole budget, the garrison calculator shows a red warning: there is no mobile garrison from points then. How much of the points of pawns from Pawn anchors in the pieces is taken from the budget. At 100% every anchored pawn replaces a bought one; at 0% anchored pawns come on top.
  • Share per ward: how the bought defenders are spread over the wards. With Share automatically by size ticked, the size of each ward decides. Otherwise set the shares of all wards but the last; the last one gets the rest up to 100%. Raising a ward takes from the last ward first, then from the others from the top.
  • With a garrison factor, the garrison entries of the wards get an active tick and a weight. Only active entries place fixed pawns from their list: min first, then extras up to max by weight while the budget lasts. The rest comes from the faction's settlement group.

  • Calculate garrison opens a calculator. Pick a faction and raid points (0, 5,000, 11,200, your current game or any number) and it shows per ward the share, the fixed pawns, the pawns from anchors, the bought pawns, how many stand on the map and how many wait in reserve, and their combat power. Pawn anchors only count with Plan the layout ticked, which plans the place once like the preview.

  • Label: the name players see.

  • Kind of place: medieval town, modern town, camp or mountain city. Click to switch to the next kind. A mountain city is planned like a medieval town and then gets a mountain over old town and suburb; its walls turn into rock.
  • Rock mantle: minimum rock thickness of that mountain. 0 means 4 for a mountain city and no mountain for the other kinds.
  • Ideoligion has / Ideoligion lacks: with Ideology, the settlement type is used only for factions whose main ideoligion has, or lacks, one of the chosen memes or precepts. Without the DLC both conditions are ignored.
  • Biomes / Not in biomes, Hilliness, Landforms / Not with landform: the settlement type only appears on tiles where all set conditions apply. Hilliness is how hilly the tile is on the world map; landforms are tile features such as coast, river, lake or valley. Empty means anywhere. If no type of the faction fits a tile, the settlement there is built without a CBF type.
  • Build as ruin: no ruin, plundered or not plundered. Every place of this type is built as a ruin, with torn-down walls, burnt rooms, partly collapsed roofs and overgrowth; the faction still lives there. Plundered leaves little loot, not plundered keeps it. A ruin set by a quest or site takes precedence.
  • Weight: how often this type is picked among all settlement types a faction can get.
  • Map size: edge length of the map this settlement type asks for, 150 to 400. The larger of this and the world setting is used.
  • Core size: edge length of the settlement core in cells, as a range. The suburb and the countryside grow around it.
  • Tree share: share of the wild trees inside the settlement that stay standing, from 0 to 1. 0 clears them all.
  • Material list: what the generator uses for walls, doors, gates, fences, roads and roofs it builds itself, see Material lists. Required: without one the row turns red and the type is switched off.
  • WD tier: only with World Domination 2.0 active. A dropdown with the tiers of World Domination, a single tier or a range such as Tier 1 to 2. The tier this type stands for, see Compatibility.
  • WD type: only with World Domination 2.0 active. The World Domination settlement types this plan stands for, such as mining camp or slave village. Only the types of the chosen tier are offered; empty means any type.
  • No WD loot: only with World Domination 2.0 active. Switches off the extra loot World Domination gives settlements of this type.
  • RIAF holding: only with A Rim of Ice and Fire active. None, Village, Town or Garrison. Makes this type the plan for holdings of that kind, see Compatibility. A type with a holding kind is not used for normal settlements.
  • Ideoligion style: only with Ideology active. Furniture gets the style of the owning faction's ideoligion.
  • Suburb areas and Suburb as ring: only for settlement types with a suburb. Suburb areas sets how many separate suburb areas grow outside the town (0 to 4). With a suburb wall with a walkway, Suburb as ring makes the suburb a closed ring; turned off, the suburb stays as separate areas on the sides.
  • Camp types: only for the kind camp. The quest sites this plan builds, such as bandit camps, outposts or work sites.
  • Tech levels: which ages the plan fits. A click switches one level on or off, a right-click picks this level and all above. Pawn lists use their members of the highest tech level at or below the settlement's level.
  • For all factions of these levels: the plan serves every faction of a ticked tech level, unless that faction has plans of its own.
  • Factions: factions or faction categories that get this plan by name.
  • Excluded: factions that never get it.
  • Mods: the mods the plan needs. Most are found automatically, + by hand adds more.
  • World map icon: the symbol of this place on the world map, tinted in the faction colour. Vanilla keeps the faction's own symbol.

How these fields decide which faction gets which settlement is explained on Factions.

Wards

A ward is one ring of the settlement: the old town behind the high wall, the suburb behind a lighter one, the countryside around them. The innermost ward is protected best and filled first.

In the preview, Wards colours the three rings: the old town red, the suburb ochre and the countryside green. Each settlement type has at most one ward of each kind, and the kind of a ward cannot be changed, see What you can change. The + next to the ward tabs adds a ward the type does not have yet.

Each ward tab has the sections Fortification, Garrison, Districts and Roads and ground.

Old town shapes

Only in the old town of a medieval settlement type. Each picture shows one outline the old town can take, for example rectangle, L-shape, cross or the grown form. By default only the grown form is selected. Click a shape to allow it, click again to exclude it. At least one shape stays selected. In XML this is the field coreShapes of the settlement type; a list of the values Grown, Rectangle, LShape, TShape, UShape, Cross, Step and Octagon. Empty means Grown only, and a name that is not in this list is reported as a config error.

Fortification

  • Walls: the wall pieces of this ring. Several entries give a wall that varies along its length; the slider sets each entry's share.
  • Emplacements: towers and other works in the ring. The generator spaces them itself, corners first.
  • Gatehouses: built wherever a road crosses the wall.
  • Door: the door that closes the passages in the wall. Empty uses the door of the material list.

The countryside has no fortification ring. Modern towns can use defensive boundaries such as sandbags, but do not use medieval gatehouses.

Obstacles outside the ward

Outworks place small obstacles around the outside of a wall or open ward boundary. They use 1×1 pieces, with settings for distance, intervals between groups and clearance around gates. Use the ward’s outworks entries in XML; Outworks includes an example. These obstacles are separate from the defensive positions distributed along roads inside the ward.

Garrison

The garrison is the set of defenders of this ward. Each row names a pawn list (a set of pawn kinds), with a min and max count and a post: Inside, Wall or Gate. The section header shows the point value of the garrison.

A ward with a garrison ignores the defender points of the settlement type. A ward without entries takes its share of those points. Pawns from Pawn anchors in the pieces come on top.

How the defenders behave:

  • Every ward defends itself. Defenders do not gather in the center and do not storm out together.
  • Guards on the wall and at the gates hold their spot, shooters stay at the embrasures.
  • Crews of ballistas, catapults and trebuchets stay at their gun, even when many defenders have fallen.
  • Defenders of a settlement cannot be recruited as prisoners.

Defences

Defences spread over the whole ward: turret posts, sandbag nests, bunkers. Each entry names a category or one piece and a count. The first one stands at a random spot along a road; every further one goes where it is farthest from those already placed. Use districts instead when a defence belongs to one district only.

The editor has no field for defences. They are set in XML in the ward's defenses list, see the ward field reference.

Districts

Use a district to keep related buildings together, such as workshops around a market or a farmhouse beside its fields. Districts without a name of their own are numbered, District 1, District 2 and so on.

  1. Buildings: each entry is a category (marked Cat.) or one specific piece, with min and max for how many. A single piece also has a weight (Wt.) against the other pieces of its category; for a category entry the pieces' own weights decide. An entry marked Filler may be built beyond its max to fill the space left at the end, and only half of its min is required. Priority (Low to Critical, default Normal) decides which entry gets space first; with equal priority the previous order applies. Low entries may stay below their min when space runs out.
  2. Field: a crop plot laid out around the buildings named under Farmsteads. The plants come from single plants or from a Crop list, see Loot. Fields only work in the suburb and the countryside.
  3. Pen: animals from a pawn list, the Herd size, and what grows inside (Growth, Soil). Pens only work in the countryside.
  4. Mine: a tunnel with chambers dug into the rock from a building of the district. You set the ore and its share, length and width of the tunnel, number and size of the chambers, rock chunks, the buildings it may start at (Starts at), and the things inside. Things opens a window with a sketch of the mine and one column per place: Entrance (the first cells of the tunnel, right behind the entrance at the hut), Tunnel (along the tunnels, only where there is room to pass) and Chamber (in a chamber at the end of a tunnel, small things by the wall). Each entry is a thing or an object list, with material (empty takes stone or wood of the map), count, and optional content with an amount, for example iron on a shelf. Things that stack, such as steel or wood, are placed as one stack of that count. ⇄ moves an entry to another place.
  5. Location of the district: tick where the district should start: Near the center, Near the gate, Near the wall or Main road. Several ticks count equally; the district looks for a spot that suits all of them. Nothing ticked means a random spot away from the other districts. In XML the fields are numbers; only their ratio counts, so 1 and 0 cover every normal case.

+ Building, + Field, + Pen and + Mine add rows. + District below the last district adds a new one. The … button next to a category shows the pieces it covers.

Roads and ground

  • Ground: the ground between the houses. Empty keeps the map's own ground.
  • Paths: the surface of lanes, squares and gate passages in this ward. Empty uses the standard surface.
  • Kerb: the surface of the outermost cells of lanes, squares and gate passages. Empty uses the path surface for the whole width.

Conditions on entries

Every building entry of a district starts with a chain icon. Open and grey means no condition. Click it to make the entry depend on other buildings: pick pieces or categories, then choose how many of them must stand, for example At least 1 of 2. The chain then closes. The whole settlement counts, and each name counts once. A smithy can so appear only where a mine or a charcoal kiln stands.

Each link gets its own colour, picked automatically from a fixed series of 18 colours in the order of the entries, so it stays the same after reloading. With more links the colours repeat with a small number in front, such as 2·. The colour is only for display and is not saved. The chain and the 2 of 3 box of the dependent entry and the for … marks of the entries it needs share that colour, across all districts. Hover over a dependent entry to light up the entries it needs. Red is kept for errors: a count that does not fit the number of names, an unknown name, or entries that need each other in a cycle.

Conditional entries are planned after the others. If the condition is not met, the entry is dropped, even with priority Critical. Dungeons have no conditions. See Modding settlements for the XML fields requires and requiresCount.

Ideoligion conditions on entries

With Ideology active, every building entry of a district has a small I button. It adds memes or precepts the owning faction's ideoligion must have (only with) or must not have (not with). The entry is then only built when the condition holds, for example a slaughterhouse only for factions with a meat preference. The button is highlighted when a condition is set; its tooltip lists them.
